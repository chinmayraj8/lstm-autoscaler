## What's confirmed so far (updated after Step 12)
- **A multi-step-aware decision engine (Step 12) doesn't overturn Step 11, but it does move the LSTM-vs-ARIMA picture.** Judged against Reactive (the project's primary metric), the multistep engine barely changes anything: same 3 machines where Reactive wins outright, forecaster-general bucket 9/13→8/13. Judged head-to-head against ARIMA specifically (same tuning rigor, same grids, both under the new engine), the LSTM's confirmed cost advantage over ARIMA rises from 1/13 (m_2085, itself a known confound) to 5/13, including 2 outright reversals (m_2189, m_2163: ARIMA won under the old engine, LSTM wins under the new one). Mechanistically sensible — the LSTM predicts the full horizon from one nonlinear function while ARIMA's forecast reverts toward the mean as it extrapolates — but the effect isn't broad enough to change who beats Reactive. ARIMA still wins outright on 6/13 machines under the improved engine.
- **Step 8's "LSTM cost advantage generalizes (10/13)" is retracted as an LSTM-specific claim (Step 11)** — see strikethrough entry below. The cost advantage over Reactive itself reproduces exactly (10/13, same machines) — what's retracted is attributing it to the LSTM. ARIMA(2,0,1), tuned with equal rigor through the identical decision engine, matches or beats the LSTM's cost on 9 of those 10 machines (confirmed cheaper on 6/9). The 10th (m_2085) is explained by Step 10 as a Reactive tuning-generalization artifact, not an LSTM mechanism. **Net finding: this project has not yet found a machine where the LSTM's cost advantage over Reactive both (a) is specific to the LSTM rather than "any reasonable forecaster," and (b) survives a known-confound check.** What robustly holds: a forecast-driven decision engine (LSTM *or* ARIMA) beats pure-reactive control on cost at moderate-to-high burstiness; that part of Step 8 stands.
- **ARIMA wired into the actual simulation (Step 10)**: no longer forecast-RMSE-only. On m_2189 (Step 8's clearest LSTM cost-advantage machine), ARIMA beats the LSTM on both cost and SLA using the identical decision engine/simulator. On m_2134 (most bursty), the LSTM narrowly beats ARIMA but both lose decisively to Reactive, unchanged from Step 8. On m_1933 (smooth), ARIMA and LSTM converge to nearly identical autoscaling decisions (differ on 4/453 test ticks).
- **m_2085 anomaly (Step 8) explained, not just observed (Step 10)**: it is a tuning-generalization artifact, not a demand-shape effect. Reactive's validation-tuned threshold (90/10) is legitimately val-optimal (rank 1/49) but generalizes catastrophically to test because m_2085's test-split demand ceiling grows 26.6% past its val-split ceiling (its cluster-mates saw +21.7% and +0.0%). Under the cluster's shared threshold (60/40), Reactive on m_2085's *actual test demand* beats the LSTM's actual test performance on both cost and SLA. The LSTM's own tuning shows a smaller version of the same val→test generalization gap.
- Scaler leakage bug: **fixed** (§7.1 of the audit).

## What's still open (in rough order)
- ~~The decision engine only ever uses max(forecast horizon), throwing away the shape/timing of the other steps~~ — **addressed (Step 12)**: `_build_multistep_targets` added alongside the original; see the "multistep" entry above for what changed and what didn't.
- The 4 new/strengthened LSTM-vs-ARIMA advantages under the multistep engine (m_2380, m_2189, m_2163, m_2183, Step 12) have not been checked for a tuning-generalization confound the way Step 10 checked m_2085 — that check materially changed the interpretation of one machine already, so these four deserve the same scrutiny before being treated as clean evidence of an LSTM forecasting-shape advantage.
- `horizon_weights` (the multistep engine's per-step weighting, Step 12) defaults to a uniform mean and was never varied — weighting the near-term step more heavily (the one that actually determines next-tick SLA risk, given `SIM_STARTUP_DELAY=1`) was not tried.
- ~~ARIMA-in-simulation run on 3 machines only~~ — **done (Step 11)**: all 13 feasible Step 8 machines now have ARIMA run through the identical decision engine/simulator.
- ARIMA's order (2,0,1) reused unchanged across all 13 machines (and again in Step 12 under the multistep engine), never re-selected per machine (e.g. via AIC) — a machine-specific order might close some of the 6 machines it still loses under multistep, or open up more LSTM wins; direction unknown.
- Whether the "val→test demand-ceiling growth" mechanism that explains m_2085 (Step 10) predicts *other* machines' anomalous verdicts among Step 8's 13 has not been checked — would need computing that feature for all 13 and correlating with verdict flips.
- SLA was reported for context in Step 11 (Reactive wins SLA on 12/13 machines, all but m_2189 where ARIMA is lowest) and again in Step 12 (moves in both directions for both forecasters under multistep, no clean pattern) but neither was run through the same three-way "confirmed" classification the cost analysis used.
- Whether a different classical baseline (exponential smoothing, Prophet) or a smaller LSTM would show the same patterns from Steps 11-12, or whether the current 128-unit 2-layer LSTM is simply over-provisioned for how easy this forecasting task is, is unexplored.
- **Training non-determinism**: re-running the same machine/seed can shift tuned params and flip close verdicts (m_2189 SLA tied → Reactive-wins between Step 5 and Step 8). Treat single-machine verdicts near a tie as directional, not exact; aggregate patterns across many machines are more trustworthy.
- **Tuning-generalization gap (Step 10, new)**: a single train/val/test split with no cross-validation on the Reactive threshold / LSTM safety-margin search can produce a validation-optimal choice that generalizes poorly to test, more severely for Reactive than for the LSTM on the one machine (m_2085) where this was directly measured. A second, distinct reason (alongside training non-determinism) to distrust single-machine verdicts near a tie.
- Cluster 1 (m_2101 and neighbors) infeasible at any reasonable scale — needs a higher `max_servers` ceiling or a burst-absorbing capacity tier to evaluate at all.
- Only 13/733 qualifying machines evaluated (1.8%) — K-means clusters are coarse over 3 features; within-cluster diversity (cluster 2 alone has 3 distinct outcomes) suggests even 13 may undersample the real variance.
- Burstiness hypothesis fully disconfirmed for SLA; fails for cost too now (correlation collapsed with more data).
